About this page

Unofficial projections, not affiliated with any news organisation or forecaster; official results come from state election offices.

Democratic control

The big number is the Democrats' chance of control, as in the chart under it; next to it, the same chance if the shared polling miss turns out one standard deviation toward either party (about 2.9 points before any votes, less as the counts pin it down). A wide range means the outcome hinges on how far off the polls are.

Before votes come in, the chance rests on the polling averages (polls up to the latest refresh), which are refreshed each morning until 3 November. Each refresh is logged in the Event log. Another forecaster's race margins can be added as a second starting point (none at present: Decision Desk HQ's was dropped on 27 October because a quarter of it is market prices); once votes are counted, each starting point is then weighted by how well its margins have predicted the counts so far, and the Count vs polls box shows the weights.

Adjustments to the polling averages with Republicans beating their polls in red states again in mind (Nate Silver's worry the week before). The averages already move each race toward Republicans by 0.18 points per point of its 2024 presidential lean toward Republicans (polls have overstated Democrats in redder states in every Senate cycle since 2000). A term letting this year's skew be larger or smaller was tried and switched off the same evening: the skew has been steady from cycle to cycle once sampling noise is allowed for, and a backtest of 2000–2022 was no better with it. What remains is that Maine starts 1.5 points closer to Collins: she has beaten her final polls by 6.5 to 8.6 points beyond each year's average miss in 2008, 2014 and 2020 (though not in 2002), but across 127 Senate candidates who ran more than once such misses have not carried over on average, so her record is shrunk to a small adjustment.

The chance Democrats hold 51 or more seats. Osborn (Nebraska, independent) is counted as a Democrat, as Silver Bulletin does: he says he will caucus with neither party, but he is not expected to side with Republicans. At 50 seats each, Vice President Vance breaks ties, so Republicans control.

Markets: the Democratic price on Polymarket (mid price), shown for comparison. It settles on a slightly narrower event than the model: Polymarket counts an independent for a party only if he caucuses with it or says he will, so if Osborn's seat is the 51st and he stays uncommitted, it may pay Republicans while the model counts Democrats. The amber marker on the bar is where the market is, labelled underneath; hovering over the bar or the label shows the market, the model and the gap between them.

"Democrats need" counts the 42 seats that are not up or safe and not modelled (one more or fewer once a safe seat is called for the other side), plus the races the model has called, against 51. The squares are the 17 races the model follows: solid once called, otherwise shaded toward whoever leads, darker the surer the model is, and ordered from the most Democratic on the left to the most Republican on the right; a tick marks a race that has been called, its letters in semi-bold. Left of the dashed line are the open races Democrats are likeliest to win, as many as they still need: win all of those and they reach 51, so the race just left of the line is the tipping point. Hovering over a square shows that race's chance and how likely it is to decide control. The same need reads as a net gain: the Democrats held 47 seats before the election (13 of the 35 up), so 51 means a net gain of 4; "expected" is the model's expected seats minus 47; once a seat is called for a party that did not hold it (a flip: the model's call, or CivicAPI's; Osborn counted with the Democrats) the line becomes "Net gain +2 of the 4 needed". On the Map and in The count so far a corner in the colour of the party holding a seat marks one changing hands (on the Map: the leader, or the winner once called); the Calls and the Event log say "D gain" or "Democratic gain"; the races table says who holds each seat.

Markets are shown and feed the momentum score; they never enter the projection.

Democratic seats

The chance of each Democratic seat total after the election, Osborn included. Democrats start from 42: 34 of their seats are not up this year and 8 of the seats up are safe enough not to model (Colorado, Delaware, Illinois, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New Mexico, Oregon, Rhode Island). The 17 races the model follows decide the rest; 10 safe Republican seats are not modelled either. If CivicAPI calls one of the 18 safe seats for the other side, the seat count moves it from then on: a safe Republican seat lost counts for the Democrats, whoever wins it (an independent too, as Osborn is), a safe Democratic seat lost counts against them. A safe seat whose party only trails changes nothing until it is called; it is flagged in the table and in What stands out. Blue bars are Democratic control (51 or more); red bars are Republican control, including 50, where the Vice President's vote decides.

Races the model has called are fixed in the simulation. The chart over time shows the expected number and its 90% range.

Count vs polls

How far the votes counted so far, taken together, run from the pre-election poll averages, in points of margin. "D +2" means the counts point to final margins 2 points more Democratic than the polls said, in every race. This shared term is how a count in one state moves the projection in all the others, in proportion to how informative it is. When another forecaster's race margins are loaded as a second starting point, a line under the number says which one the night follows: the counts shift weight to whichever's margins they fit better, which takes several races with a good part of their vote counted. A second, smaller shared term covers the votes still out: each race's remaining votes are projected from the ones already counted, and a misreading there (of how late mail or provisional ballots lean, say) tends to go the same way in every state. So the races' remaining uncertainty partly moves together; that links them without widening any single race's range.

The band is the 90% range of that shared shift; before any votes it shows how far Senate polls have missed in the past. Each dot is one race: where its count alone puts the final margin, minus its poll average. Bigger dots have more of their vote counted. Early counts get less weight than their own range suggests, because the first votes counted are often unrepresentative (vote type, which counties report first).

Counties: how the vote is moving

Every county the night reports, in any race the service reads: the 17 Senate races the model follows and, from CivicAPI, the governor and other Senate races, plus the House races of the five states with no statewide race (Indiana, Missouri, North Dakota, Utah, Washington). Each county shows its Senate race where there is one, else governor, other Senate or House. Maine and New Hampshire count the Senate race by town, so their counties show another race where CivicAPI reports one by county. Alaska (no counties; it counts by state house district) and Connecticut (results by town and old county, census figures by its new planning regions) are drawn as one unit each, the whole state: on the map and in a county's card, but not in the groups, the pattern or the surprises, which need several counties in a race.

The box above the map finds a county, a state, or a city or town (29,700 of them, each opening the county it lies in; a place in several counties opens the one named after it, else the most populous); zoomed into a state, it looks in that state only (name another state to look there); press / from any tab to type in it. One switch, the measure, sets the whole tab: the map's colours and the panel beside it. Opening a state (Counties → in a race, a race in the Still out list, or a county's card) zooms the map to it: the title becomes the trail United States → the state, its first part goes back, and the panel beside shows the county table of the state's Senate race. Swing: each finished county's move since the 2024 presidential vote (before results, 2020 → 2024). Margin: the margin so far (before results, 2024). Flips: the finished counties whose leader is not the party that won them for president in 2024, solid in the new leader's colour, the rest pale in their own (before results, the counties that flipped from 2020 to 2024); By county lists them each way, largest first; By group gives each group's share of its counties flipped each way (counties, not people). Still out: the share of each county's vote still to count (in the list, each Senate race's votes still out). Turnout rate (the share of a county's citizens of voting age who voted) and Turnout change (against the last election, relative to the rest of the race; before results 2024 against 2020, relative to the state), and Surprise once counties finish. The panel has two views. By county: the counties (or races) behind the measure, ranked: the biggest movers against their race, the largest counts so far, each Senate race's votes still out (with the make-up effect of a modelled race's uncounted counties; for the safe seats, against an estimated expected vote), the turnout furthest from its race, or the surprises; click one for its card. By group: each trait as a small column chart, its groups in order from left to right (the lowest share to the highest; big cities to rural), each column that group's figure on the measure (up: more Democratic, or higher turnout; for Still out, the share still out). A trait opens its groups as rows (click a row to highlight its counties on the map; the rest are drawn like counties with no data) and the chart of every county by that trait, where the short grey line across each group's band is its vote-weighted average: click another group's band to switch to it, the chosen one again for all groups, or a dot for its county. With all groups, the line is the trend across the trait (a vote-weighted local linear fit; for type of place, the groups' averages joined up) and, dashed behind it, the same line for the earlier cycle over the same counties (swing: 2020→2024 on the night, 2016→2020 before results; margin: 2024 on the night, 2020 before results), so a change in the pattern shows as the two lines parting. At the foot of By group, the model's own county pattern (below), which does not change with the measure. Before results the chart and its list cover every county in the country, each against its own state.

Who is moving. Counties are grouped by type of place (the CDC's 2023 urban–rural classes: big cities, their suburbs, medium and small metros, small towns, rural), education, race and ethnicity, foreign-born share, income, age, religion (2020 US Religion Census; religious bodies grouped into traditions approximately) and density. For each group the bar is its finished counties' move since the 2024 presidential vote, minus the average move of the finished counties in the same race, weighted by votes: a race's own candidates and its statewide swing drop out, leaving who moves more or less than the rest. Two marks in a lighter grey show the earlier four-year steps of the same counties: the hollow circle is always the step before the bar, the diamond the one before that. On the night the bar is tonight, the circle 2020→2024 and the diamond 2016→2020, each against the same finished counties; so a bar beyond the circle is a move that is speeding up, one short of it a move that is fading. Before any county finishes, the bar is 2020→2024 and the circle 2016→2020, each against the state; the map shows 2020→2024. Only finished counties count, because a part-counted county leans to whichever ballots it counted first. These are counties, not voters: Hispanic counties moving left does not mean Hispanic voters did.

Surprise (map and side panel): a finished county against what its race, its 2024 lean and its make-up predict, with the trait slopes from the other states' finished counties and the level from its own race (points of margin; positive is more Democratic than expected).

Still to count: in the Senate races counted by county (the modelled ones, and the safe seats with an expected vote estimated from each county's 2024 presidential vote and the race's turnout so far, a typical midterm's 70% until enough counties finish), the share of the votes still out in each group against the share already counted. How much the make-up of each race's uncounted counties moves its projection, compared with those votes swinging like the state's average county, is listed under By state when the measure is Still out. Turnout: votes for the two main candidates in a finished county as a share of its 2024 presidential vote, against its race as a whole.

Model pattern (other things equal): the model's own county pattern, below.

How the counties that have finished counting are moving from the 2024 presidential result, by their make-up, each measure with the others held equal. Every statewide race tonight counts toward it: the 17 Senate races the model follows and, from CivicAPI, the governor and other Senate races, plus the House races of the five states with no statewide race (Indiana, Missouri, North Dakota, Utah, Washington). Within each state, with an allowance for each race's own candidates, a county's move is set against its 2024 result and eight measures: share of adults with a college degree, Black, Hispanic and foreign-born residents, median household income and share aged 65 and over (Census survey, 2020–24), rural residents (2010 census) and evangelical Protestants (2020 US Religion Census). Each row is the move, in points of margin, for a county that is one typical step (one standard deviation) above average on that measure, others equal; the thin line is its 90% range. The eight were chosen by the backtest below: foreign-born and evangelical shares improved it, while white non-college share, population density, Asian share and young adults did not.

The model uses it to read the counties a state has not counted yet: if Hispanic counties elsewhere are moving toward Republicans, a state whose Hispanic counties are still out expects them to move the same way, until its own counts say otherwise. Each state's pattern starts from every other state's and is updated by its own count. It does not move a state's result against its polls, and the gap between states' patterns is kept as uncertainty. House districts that split a county are left out, since only part of the county votes in them. Maine, New Hampshire (towns) and Alaska (districts) have no census measures here and read their count as before.

In a backtest on the 2016–20 and 2020–24 county swings, replayed in this year's poll-closing order, a pattern learned this way cut the error of a state's projection, with about 60% of its vote still out, from 1.8–3.0 to 1.1–1.6 points.

Momentum

Which way the night is moving, from −3 (strongly toward Republicans) to +3 (strongly toward Democrats), over the last 15 minutes in the 2-hour view, the last hour in Tonight (12 h before the night) and the last 24 hours in 7 days. It averages two parts with equal weight, each the change over the window scored against how much that series normally moves:

Markets: the Polymarket Senate control price. Model: the change in the model's chance of control, against how far a calibrated model should move given what was counted in the window; it starts after the first polls close, so before then momentum is the markets alone.

Each part is capped at ±3. It stays off until it has a full window of live data, so a pause or a wild first count never becomes the baseline, and a market part also waits until there is enough history to know how much that price normally moves (about 45 minutes for the 15-minute window, 3 hours for the hour, 3 days for 24 hours: the markets' price history from before this service started covers it). In 7 days the model part starts a full day after the first polls close, so on election night that view is the markets alone. Under ±0.5 reads "No clear direction", 0.5 to 1.5 "Leaning to …", and 1.5 or more "Strongly to …".

Map

A tile map of the 35 races: one square per state, every seat the same size, roughly where the state is. Colour shows who is ahead and how sure: blue for the Democrat (teal for an independent: Osborn in Nebraska; Idaho, Montana and South Dakota among the safe seats), red for the Republican, deeper the surer, on a continuous scale up to about three-quarters of the party colour, so the text reads on every tile (the legend shows samples of it). For the 17 races the model projects it is the model's chance; for the 18 seats the model takes as given it is Polymarket's, on the same scale. The corner, in the colour of the party holding the seat, marks a race changing hands (Flipping or Flipped). A called race goes a touch deeper, with dark lines across it and a tick. The race you have open is outlined outside its square. Each square also shows the favourite's chance, a faint bar along the bottom for the share counted (on a fainter track once the count starts), and "!" for a warning (calls that disagree, a data check, drift, or a safe seat whose party trails or has lost it). States with no race this year are left blank. The choices in the RACES band turn the states outside the choice a flat grey, with only their letters; All, the default, shows every state. Hovering a state lights up its row in the table and the other way round; clicking a state opens the race, clicking it again closes it. The strip of races under Democratic control on the National tab uses the same colours.

A race

One race, opened from the table or the map. The browser's Back and Forward step between places: the tab, an opened race, the state and county in Counties (not filters, sorts or hovers); Back from the first place leaves the page. Along the top: Chance (the model's, with the markets' price under it), Projected margin (with its 90% range), Counted (the share of the expected vote, and the votes still out), Margin so far (with the poll average under it) and Vs polls (the projected margin against the poll average, the table's column). Once the model or CivicAPI calls the race, the title row says so ("Called for X (defeated Y) by the model at …") in the winner's colour, the tiles sit on a tint of it, and Chance becomes the chance before results, faded. A race the model does not project shows the market (before results, once called) and the margin so far. The two charts follow the chance and the projected margin through the night; the dashed line is the pre-election poll average. Left to count: the votes still out, of about how many expected, and by type of vote, each with the way it leans. What it takes: how far the trailing candidate is behind in the votes counted so far, and what share of the votes still out they would need to draw level, against the share the count so far points to. Where the votes still out are: by type of place, and how much their make-up moves the projection against a uniform swing. Largest counties still out: with their margin so far; click one to open it in Counties (a county's card; in Maine and New Hampshire the town lit on the map). At the foot, if there is room, the race's events, newest first. Counties → opens the state's county map; any choice in the RACES band above (or Esc, the 2 key, or the States tab) goes back to the list.

Races

One table lists all 35 Senate races. The RACES band groups them by the forecasters' ratings, fixed for the night (the live Model projection grouping was dropped on 2 November: the words after a state's name say 'flipping' or 'D gain' instead): the 12 forecasters' ratings as of 3 November (Cook, DDHQ, The Economist, Fifty Plus One, Fox, Inside Elections, MS NOW, NBC, RCP, Sabato, Silver Bulletin, Split Ticket; read once from Wikipedia), each race by their middle rating (between two, the firmer): Toss-up, Lean, Likely, Solid; then Surprises, the races rated Lean, Likely or Solid that were called for the other side, or are not yet called with the other side ahead now (the rows with an arrow in the Forecasters column). All, first, lists every race. Hover a state (or a row's chance) for its chance before results, the model's and the market's, and the forecasters' middle rating with Cook's, Inside Elections' and Sabato's. Shift+1 to 9 pick the choices in order. The map follows the choice, and both the grouping and the choice are remembered in this browser. With every race listed, Model projection orders them flipping to clear, then flipped and held; Forecaster ratings orders them toss-up to solid. Sorting by a column orders every listed race by it. A race you have opened stays listed whatever the choice until you close it: "Close all" above the table, or the Esc key.

On a wide screen the table sits to the left of the map and the calls; it then leaves out Votes (in the opened race) and Call (a tick by the winner's name instead; hover it for who called it), and an opened race is laid out in one column. The table scrolls in its own box, its column headings staying at the top; the map and the calls stay beside it. On a narrower screen the map goes above the full table.

The 18 Senate races the model does not follow are safe seats in the seat count (8 Democratic, 10 Republican; "safe D" or "safe R" by the name). Their rows show CivicAPI's count as it stands: its share of precincts reporting, the votes counted, the margin so far between the two main candidates (in Idaho, Montana and South Dakota, the Republican and the main independent challenger, in teal; Idaho and South Dakota have no Democrat), and CivicAPI's call. Their Chance is Polymarket's price for the favourite ("market" under it), polled every 5 minutes: a market's chance of winning, not a model projection. Nothing in these rows feeds the model. A warning under its margin (in place of "counted so far") marks a safe seat whose party trails by 3 points or more once 40% of precincts have reported and a quarter of the state's 2024 vote is counted (early counts can lean one way), and a CivicAPI call against it, which would leave the model's seat count off by that seat; both also go to the warnings and the event log. Opened, a safe seat looks like a modelled race without the model: the market's chance only, the counted margin only, what CivicAPI says is counted (no estimate of what is still out), its CivicAPI feed and events, and its counties. In Idaho, Montana and South Dakota the county map and table put the independent in the Democrat's place (teal against Harris's 2024 result); those three stay out of the national county views and the swing pattern.

An asterisk after a name marks the incumbent: the senator holding the seat, on the ballot again (22 of the 35 races, among them three appointed senators: Husted in Ohio, Moody in Florida and Darline Graham in South Carolina, appointed in July after Lindsey Graham's death). The other 13 are open seats.

Margin is the Democrat (Osborn in Nebraska) minus the Republican, in points of all votes. The grey tick on each margin bar is the pre-election poll average. The tipping chance (in a race's Chance hover; the Tipping column made way for Forecasters on 2 November) is the chance this race is the tipping point: in each of the model's simulated outcomes, line the 17 races up from most to least Democratic; Democrats need 9 of them, so the 9th race is the one that gives them the 51st seat or holds them to 50. The figures add up to 100% across the races, and a race one side is sure to win gets close to none. Several close races move together through the shared polling miss, so even the likeliest tipping point is usually well under 20%. During the count, called races drop out: the figure then shows which of the races still open is likeliest to tip control (Democrats need the 9 minus the races called for them), and once the calls decide control it is empty. "Votes" shows votes counted of the expected total (with its range) and, underneath, what is still out by vote type (e-day is election day). Kentucky and Virginia are safe, but their polls close first, so their counts are the night's earliest evidence on the shared shift. Their starting estimates are the 2024 presidential margin plus the national swing (no recent polls), with a wider range. "vs polls" is the projected margin minus the pre-election poll average: how far the night has moved that race so far, through its own count and through the shared shift from every other count. Sorting by it puts the biggest surprises first, either way. By default, and when sorting by "P(win)", the most competitive races come first: those where the trailing candidate still has the best chance (ties go to the likelier tipping point). Click a column heading to sort by it; a third click returns to the default.

Where both a state's own feed and CivicAPI report a county, the one whose count changed most recently is used; on equal counts the state's feed wins, because it splits votes by type.

Alaska elects by ranked choice: if nobody passes 50%, the two minor candidates (Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., not the senator, and Gerald Heikes) are eliminated on 18 November and their voters' next choices counted. Alaska's polls report that final round, so the dashboard counts the minor candidates' votes the same way: 35% to Sullivan, 20% to Peltola, the rest exhausted, as minor candidate John Howe's votes split when Peltola faced one Republican for the House in 2024. Alaska also counts absentee ballots for two weeks, so its count is never complete on the night.

Ohio and Florida publish each county's early and mail ballots before the night, so the model knows how much of a county's first batch is early votes instead of assuming a share. Florida also publishes them by the voter's registered party, along with its registration by party. From the two, the model sets how much more Democratic early and mail votes should run than election day, statewide and in each county (registered Democrats taken to vote 90% Democratic, Republicans 7%, others with their county's lean, and turnout by party as in the 2022 midterm). This does not forecast the result; it tells the model how to read a count that is mostly early votes, which is what Florida's first numbers are. The race detail shows the expected gap and the gap the count so far points to.

The count so far

One column per race, all 35, in the order their polls close (the hours under the columns are in the page's time zone, this browser's own or Eastern Time, Central for a browser on Eastern: View menu). One strip: each column's grey deepens from polls open (faintest) to the next to close to closed, and fills from the bottom with the share of the expected vote counted (for the races not modelled, against an estimated expected vote); once the model or CivicAPI calls the race it turns the winner's colour with the map's lines (a corner in the holder's colour if the seat changed hands). Outlined: the races that could tip control (see Could tip), and a race called while among them keeps the outline. Under it the states' codes, a called state's semi-bold, then the poll-closing hours. Above it: how many races have closed and the next closing, the votes counted and their share of the expected vote in the races counting, and the calls by party, and Could tip: the races that could tip control. Hover a column for the race; click it to open the race.

What stands out

The night's news as it stands now, numbered by importance (1 first; left to right, then the next row), worked out afresh at every refresh from figures shown elsewhere on the page; nothing in it feeds the model. It is not a log: an item comes in when it crosses its threshold and goes when it no longer stands out (a call stays). Once shown, an item stays until its figure falls below 80% of the threshold, so a figure hovering at the line does not come and go. The number's colour is the side the news favours (grey: neither). Each item takes two lines at most; hover for all of it. What does not fit the space is left out, the least important first; the Event log keeps the calls and warnings.

Once votes are counted, "before results" means the before-results snapshot. Listed: the chance of control past 99%, or 10 points or more from before results; a race called for a candidate who had under 60% before results (a toss-up decided; under 40%, an upset), with a gain noted; a race whose favourite has changed since then (now 60% or more); a race 30% or more counted whose projection runs from its polls by 4 points or more beyond the shift all races share; that shared shift itself (Count vs polls) once it is 2 points or more and its 90% range is a point clear of zero; a safe seat whose party trails (as flagged in the table) or has lost; up to two groups of counties (Who is moving), one per measure, whose swing tonight differs from the same counties' 2020→24 swing by 3 points or more, with at least 30 finished counties from three races, 200,000 votes, and a difference three times its standard error; a race not yet called whose market price (Polymarket) is 25 points or more from the model or favours the other candidate, the two furthest at most, as a contrast. Before results: the races within 60–40, any projection that has moved 1.5 points or more over 24 hours, and markets 15 points or more from the model or favouring the other candidate (the two furthest). Races called for the expected winner are in Calls, not here. Click a race for its view, a group for its counties on the map.

Two more come first when they happen: a race the model and CivicAPI called for different winners, and a call the model has withdrawn.

Event log

The night's events, newest first, from 3 November (the days before are in the recording, not listed; an event from an earlier day than now has its date under the time): calls and withdrawn calls; warnings (calls that disagree, the data check, drift, a safe seat whose party trails or has lost); poll closings and each race's first votes; the morning polling refreshes and service restarts; and any source failing for 3 minutes or more, and its recovery. The tabs choose which kinds to show: Calls, Warnings, Night (closings, first votes, refreshes, restarts) and Sources; All shows everything. Click a state named in an event to list that state's events only (the title becomes Event log → Georgia; its first part lists them all again). Log and sources opens from the View menu, or key 4. Times are in the page's time zone, this browser's own or Eastern Time (Central for a browser on Eastern; the View menu switches).

Data sources

Every feed the service reads, and how long since each last answered. Vote counts: for each of the 17 modelled races, its state's own results site and CivicAPI (for each county the one whose count changed most recently is used); under them, CivicAPI's race list and the other races it reports (the safe seats, and the governor, Senate and House races the county views use). Markets: Polymarket prices for each race and for control, shown for comparison and never used by the model. Green: answering. Grey: waiting (a feed that is not live yet). Orange: failing; one failing for 3 minutes or more also goes to the event log. Click a group for every source in it: what it feeds, when it last answered and when its data last changed.

Calls

The model calls a race when it gives the leader at least 99.5%, the count alone agrees (at least 99%), at least 20% of the expected vote is in, the drift warning is off and the count passes the data check (its counted units follow their own past results, rather than running against them as a feed with the candidates swapped would), and all of that has held for 15 minutes. A called race is fixed in the seat simulation. A call is withdrawn if the leader's chance falls below 95%. Calls from others are shown and never used.

Compared with AP: in safe races the model is later, because AP calls many at poll close from its voter survey while the model waits for counted votes. In close races the model uses a probability threshold, so on a well-calibrated night about 1 call in 200 would be wrong; AP waits until the trailing candidate has no realistic path.

The calls are listed on the States tab under the map (and in the Event log, under Calls): newest first, except that a race the model and CivicAPI called for different winners, a safe seat lost and a withdrawn call come first. Each call is also a tick along the foot of the Democratic seats chart, in the winner's colour (hover it for the race).
